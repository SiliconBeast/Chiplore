Every chip you have ever used, from the one in your phone to the one in a microwave, is built from one idea repeated billions of times: a **logic gate**.

## Two voltages, two answers

Inside a chip, a wire is either near the supply voltage or near 0 V. We call those **1** and **0**. A gate is a small circuit, a handful of transistors (a two-input NAND uses four), that looks at its input wires and drives its output wire to 1 or 0.

<Note term="Bit">One wire that is either 0 or 1. Eight of them side by side make a byte.</Note>

Each kind of gate answers a different yes/no question:

- **AND** is 1 only when *all* its inputs are 1.
- **OR** is 1 when *any* input is 1.
- **XOR** is 1 when the inputs *differ*.
- **NOT** flips its one input.
- **NAND** is AND followed by NOT. Real chips use it everywhere because it takes the fewest transistors.

## Try it

Flip the inputs and watch each gate answer. The matching row of the truth table lights up.

<GatePlay />

A **truth table** lists every combination of inputs and the output for each. Two inputs give four rows; three inputs give eight. That's how engineers describe a gate without drawing a single transistor.

## Why this matters

Put a few gates together and you get an adder. Put adders together and you get the part of a CPU that does arithmetic. Add a way to *remember* a value, which is the next lesson, and you have everything a computer needs.
